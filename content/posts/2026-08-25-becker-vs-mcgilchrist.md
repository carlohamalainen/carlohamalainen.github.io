I'm currently reading Adam Becker's [*More Everything Forever*](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/adam-becker/more-everything-forever/9781541619593/) (2025), a
physicist's tour of the ideologies of Silicon Valley: the Singularity, AI doom,
effective altruism, longtermism, Mars settlement. Years ago I read Iain
McGilchrist's [*The Master and His Emissary*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300245929/the-master-and-his-emissary/) (2009), a psychiatrist's account of
the divided brain and how the two hemispheres construct different worlds - in
his title metaphor, the right hemisphere is the Master and the left is his
emissary.

I'm seeing eerie parallels between the two books, which is interesting given
that they were written about 16 years apart and from very different
perspectives, and Becker doesn't reference McGilchrist.

This note just captures a few commonalities that stood out to me.

McGilchrist ends his book with a thought experiment: "Let us try to imagine
what the world would look like if the left hemisphere became so far dominant"
(Conclusion). I'll use this to find corresponding ideas in Becker's book.

## 1. Death as loss of control

McGilchrist: for the left hemisphere, "death is the ultimate challenge to its
sense of control" (Conclusion).

Becker: "The dream is always the same: go to space and live forever." (opening
pages). And: "Lurking underneath all of the dreams and desires and resentment of
the tech billionaires lies a fear of death, the final loss of control" (ch. 5).

McGilchrist predicted that death would become taboo, while Becker's subjects
talk about it endlessly; I guess these are two coping strategies for a problem
that no one will ever be able to solve.

## 2. The body as machine, the person as software

McGilchrist: to the left hemisphere the body is a thing among things,
"devitalised, a 'corpse'" (ch. 2), and in its world "The body would come to be
viewed as a machine" (Conclusion).

Ray Kurzweil, quoted by Becker: "A person is a mind file. A person is a software program, a very profound one, and we have no backup" (ch. 2). 

Becker's rebuttal, which draws on embodied cognition research: "We are not just
our brains. We are our bodies, situated in our environment" (ch. 2). This is
pretty much McGilchrist's central positive thesis, that we are embodied.
McGilchrist attributes this embodied perspective to the right hemisphere.

## 3. Numbers replace people; either/or replaces degree

McGilchrist predicted that numbers "would come to replace the response to
individuals", that "Morality would come to be judged at best on the basis of
utilitarian calculation", and that "'Either/or' would tend to be substituted
for matters of degree" (Conclusion). 

Longtermism is this, arithmetically literal. Becker: "This is how the
longtermists, ultimately, are forced to see people: as numbers" (ch. 4). Toby
Ord, in his own words: "There must therefore be an amount of happiness so
valuable that it is more valuable than avoiding a million people being in
extreme agony" (quoted in ch. 4). 

And the either/or: Becker describes the rationalist worldview as "eternal
paradise or extinction, with no third option" (ch. 3).

## 4. Certainty conjured rather than earned

McGilchrist's left hemisphere "needs certainty and needs to be right", and
"prefers to make something up rather than admit to not knowing" (ch. 2); the
medical stroke literature he quotes calls the isolated left hemisphere "crippled by
naively optimistic forecasting of outcomes". 

Becker asks Ord where his famous one-in-six probability of existential
catastrophe this century comes from: "I don't have a simple recipe for
creating my number... I guess it would ultimately be like, live my experiences
or something" (ch. 4). (To be fair, Ord volunteers that the estimate "is meant
to be pretty rough", but he will still defend it.)

Kurzweil, meanwhile, rates his own forecasting accuracy at 86 percent (ch. 2).

## 5. The past dismissed for the engineered future

McGilchrist: "Cultural history and tradition, and what can be learnt from the
past, would be confidently dismissed in preparation for the systematic society
of the future, put together by human will" (Conclusion). 

Anthony Levandowski, co-founder of Google's self-driving-car project, quoted by
Becker: "I don't even know why we study history... In technology, all that
matters is tomorrow" (ch. 6).

## 6. The hall of mirrors

McGilchrist describes the left hemisphere's world as "trapped in a hall of
mirrors: it only discovers more of what it already knows" (ch. 2). 

Becker describes the endpoint of the whole programme, minds uploaded into a
simulated paradise, as "a high-definition metaverse that you can't actually
leave" (ch. 2), everything fitted to "the single Procrustean solution of code
and compute". A self-enclosed world of representations.

## Where they don't line up

I wouldn't expect a perfect correspondence, and here's a few places where they diverge:

* McGilchrist's left-hemisphere world comes with a vast expansion of
bureaucracy; Becker's subjects loathe bureaucracy and dream of escaping
regulation entirely. 
* McGilchrist predicted disenchantment - "Religion would seem to be mere
fantasy" (Conclusion) - while Becker documents the opposite: an accidental machine religion,
transhumanism as "an outgrowth of Christian eschatology" (ch. 6). 
* Becker is a scientific materialist, and McGilchrist files scientific
materialism itself under left-hemisphere products. I think they would probably argue with each other?
* Becker, on humanity's biggest problems: "They're social problems, requiring
social solutions. ... Nothing is coming to save us" (ch. 6). McGilchrist would
say that we need to recover the kind of attention we pay to the world.

## Conclusion 

McGilchrist closes his book, in 2009, with: "This is what the world would look like
if the emissary betrayed the Master. It's hard to resist the conclusion that
his goal is within sight."

I feel like this is where the world is headed, and - at least in McGilchrist's
terms - the AI/LLM boom will only accelerate it.
